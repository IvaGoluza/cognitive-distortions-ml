Looking at the validation metrics: recall is 0.28 with precision 0.06. The model is generating roughly 5× too many predicted span-tokens, which means it is identifying distorted regions, just over-extending them or fragmenting them. Strict F1 punishes both fragmentation (one gold span split into three predictions = three FPs and one FN) and over-extension (predicted span 5 tokens longer than gold = full FN under strict)

A second issue worth ruling out: the boundary detection itself may be hampered by class imbalance on B-DIST specifically.
With weight 8.0 on B-DIST (1,258 train tokens) versus 2.46 on I-DIST (48,362 tokens), the model has a much weaker signal for "where does a span start" than "is this token inside a span". This is the standard NER-with-rare-Bs problem.
A simple intervention: weighting B-DIST more aggressively.

The dataset's spans are highlighted by therapists from full patient questions. Inter-annotator agreement was reported at 61% in Shreevastava & Foltz, which is low — therapists frequently disagree about exactly where a distorted thought begins and ends. Strict span F1 requires the model to predict a span with exactly the same character/token boundaries as the human annotator. If human annotators don't even agree with each other on boundaries, a model trained on noisy labels can't be expected to predict them exactly either.
This is why most NER evaluation reports both strict and partial/lenient matching. The seqeval library has a default mode that does partial matching (any overlap = match) which is much closer to what's clinically meaningful — knowing roughly where the distortion is, even if the exact boundaries differ by a few tokens.

The model's recall is 0.27 (it identifies ≈27% of gold spans with exact boundary match),
and precision is around 0.05–0.10 (most of what it predicts isn't matched).
A precision of 0.05 means ≈19 of every 20 predicted spans don't match a gold span exactly.
With 273 gold spans in test and recall 0.27, the model finds 73 correct spans — but it predicts roughly 750 spans total (since 73 / 0.10 ≈ 730).
The model is massively over-predicting: producing many short fragmented spans and many over-extended ones, both of which fail strict matching.

The high B-DIST weight (8.0) is too aggressive for a binary task. The model has learned to scatter B-DIST predictions
liberally because the loss heavily rewards finding any boundary token. Combined with no category to coordinate, the model produces noisy fragmented output.
Next step is to drop the weights. The 3C task has enough positive signal in I-DIST (48k tokens, 13% of training) that the model doesn't need aggressive weighting.
Try weights closer to natural frequencies, with only modest boost on B-DIST.

SUMMARY
The joint 3B BIO tagger experiment yielded F1 strict 0.032; switching to binary 3C span detection improved performance
modestly to F1 strict ≈ 0.12 with recall ≈ 0.20, indicating the model identifies approximate locations of distorted
thinking but does not consistently reproduce annotator-assigned span boundaries.
The reported inter-annotator agreement of 61% on TherapistQA is likely a contributing factor,
since strict boundary matching is sensitive to the variability inherent in expert span annotation.

Recall = 0.50. For every gold distortion span in the test set, the model overlaps with it half the time. Half of all annotator-marked distortions are at least partially recovered by the model.
Precision = 0.26. Of every 100 spans the model predicts, about 26 overlap a gold span. The remaining 74 are either spurious or are picking up text the annotator didn't mark as distorted but might still be distortion-related.
F1 = 0.34. Harmonic mean of the two.
The model has clearly learned to find distorted content (50% recall is real, not noise). But it's noisy in two distinct ways:
It over-predicts. Precision 0.26 means the model produces many candidate spans. Some are fragmented (Record 3 — splitting a gold span into two), some are adjacent sentences picked instead of the annotator's choice (Records 4 and 6), and some may be genuinely distorted content the annotator missed (cognitive distortions often co-occur, and Shreevastava & Foltz's annotators were instructed to mark the primary distortion).
Strict matching dramatically underestimates capability. Strict F1 = 0.13, partial F1 = 0.34 — that's a 2.6× gap. The strict metric is rejecting predictions that overlap gold but don't match exactly. Given that the human annotators themselves only agree 61% of the time on boundaries, expecting a model to exactly reproduce one annotator's choices is unreasonable.
